01 / How I approach design

Research first.
Then simplify.

Almost everything I build starts with research. I use AI-assisted research to explore a problem and compare existing solutions, look at the numbers and ask friends for different perspectives. I write out the experience I want, try it myself and explore several mockups before settling on a direction.

Then I simplify. I remove buttons and information that don't serve the main task, compare the remaining options and refine the one that works best. I often ask friends to try or look at the result and use their reactions to make another pass. I don't always make a formal empathy map for personal projects, but I do think through the user's perspective. Hick's Law captures part of that instinct: more and more complex choices can slow decisions down.

Research & framing

Gather information from AI-assisted research, conversations, existing products and available numbers. Understand the user, the task and the constraints before choosing a design.

Explore & simplify

Write out the intended flow, try mockups, test different layouts, remove unnecessary controls and compare the strongest options.

Evaluate & refine

Try the interface, ask friends for informal feedback and iterate. Review keyboard access, labels, feedback, responsive layout and recovery. Informal reactions are useful, but are not the same as a formal usability study.

My toolkit spans user research, personas and empathy mapping, ideation, wireframes, prototyping, usability heuristics and UX laws, alongside HTML, CSS, JavaScript and software engineering.

02 / Selected coursework · Fall 2026

Smart Fishing App & Advisor.

A mobile and web application concept that helps anglers record fishing trips, organize their experiences and use past information to make better decisions.

Connor Graville and I worked on this project together. I helped implement some of the interface upgrades and enjoyed working on a problem outside my usual interests. I don't know a great deal about fishing, which made learning how anglers think and what they need especially interesting.

The problem

Scattered memories.

Fishing information often ends up across photos, notes and memory. Anglers may forget which flies, locations or conditions worked, making patterns hard to find later.

The people

Anglers, guides and fly shops.

Our early research report describes five interviews. Our primary persona, Jake Miller, is a 29-year-old recreational Montana angler who wants to organize his trips and remember successful techniques.

Three central features.

01 / Quick trip logging

Capture catches, flies or lures, locations, conditions, photos and voice notes.

02 / Fishing history

Organize, search, filter and compare previous trips instead of digging through scattered records.

03 / Fishing insights

Explore successful patterns, recommended flies, favorites and recently used flies.

Additional proposed capabilities include offline access, weather information and GPS tracking. These are features of the design concept, not claims of a released production app.

From research to prototype.

  1. EmpathizeInterview anglers and understand their experiences.
  2. DefineUse research findings and a persona to frame the problem.
  3. IdeateExplore ways to organize and simplify trip logging.
  4. PrototypeWork toward a high-fidelity interactive design in Adobe XD.
  5. EvaluateUsability testing and analysis are the next evidence to add.

Small design choices with a purpose.

Fitts’s Law

A prominent Log Catch button makes the main action easy to locate and tap. The smaller Add Photo control keeps a secondary action in its place; Save and Cancel are separated.

Hick’s Law

Organized recommendations, favorites and recently used flies narrow the choices an angler has to consider.

Zeigarnik effect

Missing-data warnings and incomplete-trip indicators call attention to information that still needs to be entered. These are design intentions, not measured outcomes.

Design evolution / EasyLife

From a clunky collection of tools to a calmer assistant.

I originally built EasyLife as a personal assistant, but the first version felt clunky. I wanted the different tools to work together, rather than asking users to navigate a collection of disconnected utilities. I've continued refining its interface using what I've learned about user experience.

Earlier interface problems

Inconsistent navigation between modules, too much visual chrome, crowded controls and a desktop-like experience on smaller screens. Earlier audit notes also flagged unclear save feedback and task capture.

Documented refinement

A tighter shared navigation and spacing system, simpler Today entry point, a primary next action, a smaller capture surface and clearer visual hierarchy. A later candidate retained approximately 44px mobile targets, visible focus and reduced-motion behavior.

Design evolution / HouseOS

Different roles need different interfaces.

The original training-oriented idea grew into a broader restaurant operations system. Research into restaurant work pointed to fragmented information, delayed updates and a need for quick access to shift information and training.

Earlier design challenge

Keeping public information, staff tasks and management controls understandable in the same system without making a busy service workflow unnecessarily complicated.

Documented design changes

Separate role-specific navigation for staff, managers and guests; focused mobile menus; clearer preview labels; simpler save-status language instead of technical implementation terms. These choices support recognition, lower cognitive load and reduce the chance of navigating to the wrong surface.

Visual hierarchy

Large headlines, distinct project chapters and consistent typography establish what deserves attention first.

Progressive exploration

Featured projects give a starting point, while category filters and search help people narrow the larger collection. This also supports recognition rather than recall.

Interaction & accessibility

Interactive demos provide a way to experience an idea rather than only read about it. The site includes responsive layouts, a skip link, labeled controls and reduced-motion considerations. These features are not a claim of full accessibility conformance.
